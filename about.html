Who Is This Guy

TLDR: Tell me I am wrong — I want to learn. It has been a journey from illiterate chronic classroom disruptor to community and team collaborator. I come from a family of healers, and I am not trying to break the chain. I zigged and I zagged my way to one of the most interesting platforms in healthcare and innovation, and I want to keep zigging and zagging my way to interesting places. Above all else, I want to learn, build, and heal.

Here's the gist: I come from a family of healers. My dad spent 32 years healing people in emergency rooms. My mom spent just as long healing the community around her. My sister is training to heal people too. I don't want to miss out or break the trend.

I went to school and couldn't pick what to learn and didn't read or focus too good. At some point, I got pulled out of class from time to time and driven to an office with a nice lady who gave me cool pencil toppers after having me play a bunch of brain games. One day, the tests were over and I had to learn how to swallow a pill. Around the same time, I would get pulled out of class during Social Studies to go to the library where another lady had me read with my finger tracing the words as I went. I did not know/comprehend it at the time, but I had been diagnosed with ADHD and a reading-based learning disability.

Throughout elementary school I did well socially and athletically, but had a pretty impressive detention debt lined up for most of middle school because homework completion was not a priority of mine. I did not get to go outside during recess much those years which is a great way to manage the symptoms of ADHD. All work and no play made Brett a dull boy.

In high school I locked in. I connected with my class, athletic teams, and club members. I cared deeply about my teams and communities and pushed myself to be my best for them and tried to elevate everyone else in them. If you told me I was doing that at the time I would have gotten really embarrassed and told you "nuh-uh," then to prove my point beyond a shred of doubt I would have not studied even harder for the Mandarin quiz that was about to kick my butt. I kept that same anti-serious energy in my extracurricular activities — I wore capes before and after rivalry games and I gave myself the title "Head Master of Headlines" on the publication staff. Regardless, I was awarded California's Small School Baseball Player of the Year, Athlete of the Year, and delivered the commencement address during graduation. I wanted to be a part of excellent teams, but did not want to be seen as excellent in them. I was the perfect little communist.

When I got to Pitzer College, I was fortunate enough to continue playing the sport I loved, but I had to pick which knowledge to pursue. Naturally, I picked nothing by picking everything. I built my own Politics, Philosophy, and Economics with help from Department Chairs across the Claremont Consortium. I think I chose that major because I knew there was something sick about the world I was being pushed into after college and I wanted to understand the structure of the sickness from the main pillars building our world today. Philosophy to understand where our knowledge and ethics come from, politics to understand how power and control are established and wielded, and economics to understand the underlying machinery that steers all of it today.

I wanted to see if I could find where the disease was hiding. I wanted to see if I could find a lever we pulled or a button we pressed that led us to a world where unprecedented peace and prosperity in one place exists on the back of poverty and suffering in another. Maybe I did not want to put on a lab coat, but I did want to pick up a scalpel and see what was going on in there.

After that, I spent a year or so absolutely honing my craft on the oven at Blaze Pizza. I was whipping up the best pizzas you could get comfortably under $20. At one point, during Meatball Mania May, a month-long promotion where you could add a cup of meatballs as a side to your pizza order, I really hit my stride. I was the only one doing it in the whole store, and by the end of the month we were in the top five stores nationally for gross meatball-in-a-cup output. It was easily one of the best bits I have ever been a part of — it never failed to make me smile and/or make the customer uncomfortable. But all good things must come to an end, and my time at Blaze had an expiration date if I was to go on to heal things greater than lunchtime tummy rumbles.

That is when my parents mercifully kicked me out of the house so I could put down my roots in new soil, grow, and turn some new leaves. I moved to San Francisco, and joined the Israel Economic Mission as their new BD specialist for the Healthcare Vertical. It put me in a position to learn all about the business behind my dad's career. Finally, I could sell meatballs that actually helped people. But in all seriousness, I now sit at the intersection between life-saving people and life-saving technology connecting two of the most exciting innovation ecosystems in the world. And get to learn and grow with both. After two years I am now the Manager of Healthcare BizDev and Ventures for my office. Yes, I am a bit young, but that's when all the experts say you heal the best.

Now, I am embarking on a new journey of learning. I am reading books written in the context of healing while talking to every type of professional healer I can find.

Starting with Paul Auster's Mr. Vertigo to understand the dynamic nature of healing as well as the trust and submission required to heal physically and spiritually. John Carreyrou's Bad Blood and Clayton Christensen's The Innovator's Dilemma are next on my list to provide a framework and examples for evaluating the business of innovation within health.

Atul Gawande's Being Mortal and Siddhartha Mukherjee's The Emperor of All Maladies will help me wrestle with the moral and existential question of what it means to live and to heal and why, even though death comes for us all one day, it is noble, both in spite of and because of the futility, to fight for a more comfortable and longer life.

Lastly, I am tackling Paul Starr's The Social Transformation of American Medicine, T.R. Reid's The Healing of America, and Rick McIntyre's The Rise of Wolf 8, to compare and contrast how we care for each other in society at its most simple and most complex. I am happy to add books to my list, so please shoot me some recs. May as well go all in on this one, the subject matter is life and death after all.

I am also listening to podcasts, watching youtube, and reading blogs & research papers. Sounds like amateur hour, I know, but tell that to Mr. Khan of the so-named Academy fame. Also, Ben and David over at Acquired do some quality research and storytelling.

Above all else, I am doing this so people can tell me that I don't know what I am talking about. Hopefully, at that point, they will be so inclined as to tell me why I am wrong and I can learn a thing or two. And, maybe, in reading some of what I write, they can too. ∎
